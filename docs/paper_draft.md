## 1. Introduction

Cuneiform tablets, among the earliest surviving written records, are frequently damaged, and reconstructing their missing content is a slow, expert-dependent process: roughly half of the half a million cuneiform tablets excavated to date remain untransliterated (Cobanoglu et al. 2024). Where a tablet survives only in fragments, philologists reconstruct the missing signs by hand, drawing on their own recollection of parallel passages and formulaic phrasing — a process that is inherently subjective and does not scale to the volume of unpublished material (Lazar et al. 2021).

This reconstruction task maps directly onto masked language modeling (MLM), the pretraining objective underlying BERT (Devlin et al. 2019): both ask a model to predict a token from its surrounding context. Lazar et al. (2021) were the first to make this connection explicit for Akkadian, framing sign restoration as masked-token prediction rather than designing a bespoke generative architecture for the task.

A parallel line of work in Greek and Latin epigraphy has pursued the same idea with purpose-built architectures. Pythia (Assael et al. 2019) was the first deep-learning system for ancient-text restoration, trained from scratch on Greek inscriptions. Its successor Ithaca (Assael et al. 2022) extended restoration with two attribution tasks — assigning a damaged inscription's geographic origin and its date of writing — within one multi-task architecture. Aeneas (Assael et al. 2025) extended Ithaca further, adding a vision branch that conditions the geographic-attribution head on a photograph of the inscription, and handling gaps of unknown length. For Akkadian, Lazar et al. (2021) established that a fine-tuned multilingual encoder outperforms a from-scratch monolingual one for restoration, but did not extend the task to attribution or to a visual modality. We combine both lines of work: a fine-tuned multilingual BERT, in the spirit of Lazar et al. (2021), extended with Ithaca/Aeneas-style multi-task attribution heads (period, genre, language, provenience) and an Aeneas-style vision-conditioned head — applied to Akkadian for the first time.

Both Ithaca and Aeneas frame their systems as decision-support tools rather than autonomous replacements for philologists, and report that access to model predictions measurably improves human performance: historians given Ithaca's top-20 restoration hypotheses improved their own restoration accuracy from 25% to 72% (Assael et al. 2022). We adopt the same framing — our goal is a tool that narrows the space of plausible readings and attributions for a human expert to evaluate.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Fine-tuned BERT for Akkadian

Lazar et al. (2021) fine-tuned multilingual BERT (mBERT) on transliterated Akkadian text from the Open Richly Annotated Cuneiform Corpus (ORACC), roughly 10,000 transliterated tablets totalling 1 million words and 2.3 million signs. Predicting masked signs, their fine-tuned model reached 89.5% top-5 accuracy (MRR 0.83, Hit@5 0.89 overall); notably, mBERT's *zero-shot* performance (before any Akkadian finetuning at all) already outperformed a monolingual Akkadian BERT trained from scratch by about 10%, suggesting multilingual pretraining itself, not only the finetuning data, does much of the work. To inject Akkadian-specific vocabulary without retraining mBERT's embedding table, they repurposed 99 of mBERT's reserved but otherwise-unused `[unusedN]` vocabulary slots via the WordPiece algorithm. We adopt the same mechanism for two damage-marker tokens (Section 3) and, going further, use the remaining free slots for 97 Akkadian-specific WordPiece tokens learned directly from our own corpus.

Fetaya et al. (2020) provide direct evidence, on Akkadian administrative and legal texts, that bidirectional context is substantially more informative than left-only context for this task: an LSTM given the full surrounding context reached MRR 0.89, against MRR 0.754 for the same architecture given only the context preceding a gap. This is the strongest available justification, within the Akkadian-restoration literature itself, for a bidirectional encoder such as BERT over an autoregressive, left-to-right decoder.

### 2.2 Pythia → Ithaca → Aeneas

Pythia (Assael et al. 2019), a sequence-to-sequence BiLSTM operating on character and word representations jointly, was the first deep-learning system applied to ancient-text restoration; on a held-out set of Greek inscriptions it reached a character error rate (CER) of 30.1%, against 57.3% for expert epigraphists working under the same conditions.

Ithaca (Assael et al. 2022) restructured the task around one shared transformer torso feeding three separate task heads — restoration, geographic attribution, and chronological attribution — trained jointly on the Packard Humanities Institute's ancient Greek inscription corpus. Ithaca alone reaches 61.8% top-1 restoration accuracy (26.3% CER); for geographic attribution, among 84 candidate regions, 70.8% top-1 and 82.1% top-3 accuracy; for chronological attribution, its predictions fall a median of 3 years from the ground-truth date range. This shared-torso, per-task-head design is the direct architectural precedent for the multi-task model described in Section 4.

Aeneas (Assael et al. 2025) replaced Ithaca's torso with a T5-style decoder augmented with rotary positional embeddings, extended restoration to gaps of unknown length, and added a vision branch: a small convolutional network (ResNet-8) processes a photograph of the inscription, and its output is concatenated with the text representation feeding the geographic-attribution head only. Aeneas's authors explicitly exclude the visual input from the restoration head, to avoid the model exploiting unmasked regions of the image to infer masked text, and from the dating head, having found no measurable benefit in ablation experiments. This scoping decision is the direct precedent for the vision-conditioned head described in Section 4; we did not simply adopt it, but independently re-derived the same scoping via our own controlled ablation (Section 5).

### 2.3 Vision + cuneiform

Kapon et al. (2024) classify a cuneiform tablet's historical period from its photograph alone — no text — with a ResNet50 model trained on 94,936 CDLI tablet images, reaching 61% macro-F1 against an 8% baseline from a decision tree using only the tablet's height-to-width ratio. This shows that a tablet's physical shape carries a real, learnable period signal through a purely visual channel. Our own results (Section 5) find no corresponding benefit from adding a visual input to a *joint* text+image model's period head; one plausible reconciliation is that text alone, via the language, orthography, and genre cues an mBERT-based model already reads, already captures most of the available period signal, leaving little for a fused image feature to add — unlike Kapon et al.'s vision-only setting, where the image is the model's only available signal.

Dencker et al. (2020) show that individual cuneiform signs can be localized within tablet photographs using only weak supervision — aligning existing transliterations to tablet images, rather than requiring manually annotated bounding boxes — establishing tablet photography at CDLI's own scale as a workable input for computer-vision methods specific to cuneiform.

Mahmood and Panok (2025) apply neural machine translation to the reconstruction and translation of damaged Akkadian passages from the Epic of Gilgamesh, reporting that their system performs well on formulaic lines and passages with close parallels elsewhere in the epic, but struggles with rare vocabulary, damaged proper names, and syntactically uncertain lines. We observe the same pattern in our own results (Section 5): top-1 restoration accuracy on our curated Gilgamesh/Enuma Elish/Atrahasis/Hammurabi examples (53-55%) is markedly lower than on a random sample of the (predominantly administrative) test set (63-64%).

### 2.4 Other Akkadian NLP

Several other recent projects apply deep learning to different Akkadian-language tasks: automatic transliteration of Unicode cuneiform glyphs into words, at up to 97% accuracy (Gordin et al. 2020); neural machine translation from Akkadian to English, from both cuneiform glyphs and transliteration directly (Gutherz et al. 2023); and, most recently, bidirectional Akkadian-English translation paired with a tool for rendering composed text as cuneiform (Wang 2026). None of these address restoration or attribution. A 2025 shared task on lemmatization and token prediction for Akkadian and Sumerian (Gordin, Sahala, Spencer & Klein 2025) further confirms that restoration-style prediction is an active benchmarking target for the field.

### 2.5 Data sources

Our corpus draws on two external sources beyond ORACC's own catalogue metadata. CuneiML (Chen et al. 2023) supplies Unicode cuneiform glyph segmentations aligned to CDLI tablet photographs and bounding boxes, and is the basis of the visual input used in Section 4. The electronic Babylonian Library (eBL) publishes a growing, openly licensed (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) transliteration corpus of roughly 25,000 tablets and 350,000 lines (Cobanoglu et al. 2024); we draw on the project's Zenodo snapshot both to recover transliterations for tablets missing usable text elsewhere and for several of our curated literary examples.

## 3. Data

**Evaluation regime.** We evaluate restoration under one regime: 15% of tokens masked at random following BERT's own masking recipe, evaluated on a held-out test split never used during training or checkpoint selection. We do not additionally evaluate against real editorial reconstructions — signs an ORACC editor restored inside square brackets, as distinct from signs plainly attested on the tablet — because our text-processing pipeline does not preserve, in the final corpus, which characters originated from an editor's bracketed conjecture rather than a direct reading; recovering that distinction would require a new parsing pass over ORACC's raw markup, left to future work. Like Aeneas, we do not strip editorial conjectures from the training text either, for the same reason its authors give: excluding them would discard usable data from an already data-scarce domain (Assael et al. 2025).

**Corpus composition.** Our corpus totals 60,050 documents. The majority, 51,974, come from merging and deduplicating ORACC's own editions with CuneiML's; a further 8,076 are drawn directly from CDLI's bulk ATF export and from eBL — recovering tablets whose text existed nowhere else in our sources, balancing underrepresented period/genre/language/provenience classes, and adding a small curated set of well-known literary texts (the Epic of Gilgamesh, Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, the Laws of Hammurabi) held out into the test split. Of the full corpus, 7,049 documents (11.7%) additionally have a manually reviewed photograph; the remainder receive an all-zero placeholder image at training time, following the same design Aeneas uses for the roughly 95% of its own corpus without an image (Assael et al. 2025). Every one of these 7,049 photographs is presented to the model, but — as detailed in Section 4 — it is used by only one of the four classification heads, provenience.

**Metadata categories.** We map the free-text period, genre, provenience, and language fields recorded by CDLI and ORACC onto four small, fixed category sets by substring rather than exact match: raw catalogue values carry inconsistent suffixes (e.g. "Old Babylonian (ca. 1900-1600 BC)"), and exact matching discarded 30-58% of otherwise usable records to an "Unknown" placeholder. The resulting categories necessarily coarsen CDLI's own finer distinctions to keep each head's class count tractable at our corpus size — our genre category "Literary & Scholarly", for instance, merges CDLI's astrological, omen, ritual, mathematical, and prayer genres. The final sets: nine periods (Neo-Assyrian, Ur III, Old Babylonian, Old Assyrian, Middle Assyrian, Middle Babylonian, Neo-Babylonian, Third Millennium, Late Antiquity); six genres (Administrative, Lexical, Royal Inscriptions, Literary & Scholarly, Legal, Letters); four languages (Akkadian, Sumerian, Bilingual, Peripheral/Other); twelve provenience classes (Nineveh, Umma, Girsu, Nippur, Puzriš-Dagan, Kanesh, Assur, Uruk, Ur, Ugarit, Sippar, Nimrud).

**Missing labels.** A record whose raw value matches none of these categories, or is simply absent, is treated as missing: it is assigned label -100 and excluded from both the loss and the accuracy/F1 computation for that head, rather than defaulted to an arbitrary class. Missing-label rates vary substantially by head — 2.0% for period, 11.5% for genre, 18.1% for language, 23.5% for provenience — reflecting how consistently each field is actually populated in the underlying catalogues; provenience, with both the most classes and the least complete findspot data of the four, is the most affected.

**Text-level damage markers.** Separately from missing category labels, the transliterated text itself carries two distinct damage signals, which we map to two of mBERT's reserved token slots and exclude from both masking targets and the model's own candidate outputs, since a position marking genuinely unrecoverable content has no ground truth to train against: a single sign of uncertain reading (`x` in the source transliteration, present in 32.3% of documents and 9.26% of word-tokens), and a gap of unknown length (an ellipsis in the source transliteration, present in 16.5% of documents and 2.01% of characters). This mirrors the single-character/unknown-length distinction Aeneas encodes as `-`/`#` (Assael et al. 2025).

## 4. Methods

Our encoder is `bert-base-multilingual-cased`, fine-tuned rather than trained from scratch, for the same reason Lazar et al. (2021) give for the same choice: the available Akkadian corpus is orders of magnitude smaller than what a competitive from-scratch transformer requires. We confirm this empirically for our own corpus (Section 5): masked-token-prediction mean reciprocal rank rises from 0.512, for an otherwise identical but un-finetuned model, to 0.797 after finetuning — fine-tuning does substantial additional work beyond what mBERT's own pretraining alone provides.

Four classification heads — period, genre, language, provenience — are trained jointly with the masked-language-modeling objective on one shared encoder, following the same shared-torso, per-task-head design as Ithaca and Aeneas (Assael et al. 2022; 2025), extended from their two attribution tasks (region, date) to four; genre and language have no direct equivalent in Ithaca/Aeneas's Greek/Latin setting. Task losses are weighted 3.0 for the masked-language-modeling objective and 1.0 for each metadata head — close to, though not copied from, Aeneas's own fixed weighting of 3 for restoration, 2 for region, and 1.25 for date (Assael et al. 2025).

For tablets with a photograph, we add a vision branch: a ResNet18 backbone, initialized from ImageNet and fine-tuned jointly with the rest of the model, whose output is normalized and concatenated with the encoder's `[CLS]` representation feeding the provenience head only. This mirrors Aeneas's design of concatenating a small convolutional network's output into the geographic-attribution head alone (Assael et al. 2025), with two differences. First, we use a larger, ImageNet-pretrained backbone rather than a from-scratch ResNet-8, since our image corpus is roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than Aeneas's and a from-scratch convolutional network overfit in preliminary experiments. Second, we use lighter geometric augmentation — rotation and shear capped at 15°/5°, against Aeneas's reported 30°/10° — after finding the wider range clipped genuine tablet content on our tightly, manually reviewed image crops.

## 5. Results

**Metrics.** We report mean reciprocal rank and top-1/top-3/top-5 accuracy for masked-token restoration, matching Lazar et al. (2021)'s own metric choice for direct comparability, and accuracy alongside macro-averaged F1 for each of the four classification heads — accuracy alone can mask collapsed performance on minority classes under the severe class imbalance described in Section 3, which our untrained baseline illustrates directly (non-trivial accuracy from majority-class prediction alone, macro-F1 near zero). We do not report character error rate: CER is suited to evaluating open-ended text generation of unknown length, as in Ithaca and Aeneas; our task instead predicts a single token from a fixed vocabulary at each masked position, which the metrics above already capture directly.

**The provenience-vision effect.** This is not a single-run artifact: on the validation split (used during development, not the number reported above), the same provenience-conditioned configuration was trained twice independently and reached macro-F1 0.766 and 0.764 — 0.002 apart — against a validation-split text-only baseline of 0.725. Both independent runs clear the noise floor established using the language head, which never receives the image in any run, yet whose own macro-F1 varies by as much as 0.05 between otherwise-identical runs from random initialization alone. Repeating the same conditioning experiment for period and genre, individually and jointly, shows no corresponding effect for either: both remain within the range spanned by their own unconditioned runs. This is, to our knowledge, an independent confirmation — on a corpus roughly thirty times smaller than Aeneas's, and with a different vision backbone — of Aeneas's own finding that vision conditioning benefits geographic attribution specifically, and not other attribution tasks (Assael et al. 2025).

**Per-class breakdown.** The provenience gain is not distributed evenly across classes: it concentrates in the classes with the fewest training examples (Nimrud, F1 +0.241; Ur, +0.107; Assur, +0.037). ## 6. Conclusion

We present, to our knowledge, the first system to combine Ithaca/Aeneas-style multi-task attribution with masked-token restoration and a vision-conditioned head for Akkadian. Where Lazar et al. (2021) established restoration alone for this language, and Aeneas established vision-conditioned attribution for Latin at a substantially larger data scale, we combine both directions and add two attribution tasks — genre and language — with no direct analogue in prior epigraphic systems. Our central empirical finding — that a tablet's photograph benefits provenience attribution specifically, and not period or genre attribution — independently reproduces Aeneas's own finding for a structurally different language, script, and corpus scale, evidence that the effect reflects a genuine property of the underlying data (tablet material, shape, and photographic convention correlating with findspot) rather than an artifact of one project's pipeline. We also report, without omission, that an alternative from-scratch character-level architecture was built and evaluated for this project and found unproductive relative to fine-tuned mBERT at comparable training cost.
